Micronutrient Deficiencies and Performance Impact

High School Course 6, Lesson 5

Video Micronutrient Deficiencies and Performance Impact 2 min

Reading Micronutrient Deficiencies and its Impact on Performance 5 min

You step onto the field or court feeling “normal” during warm-ups—but by the second half of practice, your legs feel heavier than usual, your focus drifts, and plays you normally execute without thinking suddenly feel harder. You might assume it’s just a tough day, but often this kind of drop in performance is connected to something much smaller: micronutrients.

What are Micronutrients?

Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals your body needs in very small amounts. They play important roles in energy production, immune function, bone health, and muscle function. When your eating pattern includes a variety of nutrient-rich foods, these functions are generally well-supported.

Important: Symptoms are nonspecific. Fatigue, poor focus, mood changes, weakness, and slow recovery can be caused by many things — including illness, poor sleep, stress, inadequate total calories, dehydration, or overtraining. These symptoms alone do not mean you have a micronutrient deficiency. Only a healthcare professional can diagnose a deficiency through clinical assessment and lab tests.

The Role of Micronutrients

Each micronutrient plays a specific, well-established role in the body. Iron helps carry oxygen in the blood, which supports endurance. Calcium and vitamin D contribute to bone strength. B vitamins are involved in energy metabolism. However, no single nutrient works in isolation — they function together in the context of your whole diet.

Explicit prohibition on self-supplementation: Do not start taking vitamin, mineral, or other dietary supplements based on this lesson or your own symptom matching. Unnecessary supplements can be expensive, ineffective, and in some cases harmful (e.g., taking iron without a diagnosed deficiency can cause toxicity). If you are concerned about a possible deficiency, speak with a healthcare provider who can test and advise appropriately.

Food-First Prevention

The most reliable way to support your micronutrient intake is through a varied, food-first approach:

  • Fruits and vegetables of different colors
  • Whole grains
  • Lean proteins (meat, fish, eggs, beans, tofu)
  • Dairy or fortified alternatives
  • Nuts and seeds

Some groups of athletes may be at higher risk for certain nutrient shortfalls (e.g., iron for endurance athletes and menstruating athletes, vitamin D for athletes in northern climates or indoor sports). Awareness of risk factors — not symptom guessing — is the appropriate approach. A healthcare provider can help determine if your specific situation warrants testing.

If a micronutrient deficiency is diagnosed by a healthcare provider, treatment typically involves dietary changes and possibly supplements under medical supervision. The first step is always proper assessment — not self-diagnosis.

The Bottom Line

Your performance is influenced by many factors — training, sleep, stress, hydration, and overall nutrition pattern. A varied eating pattern that includes micronutrient-rich foods supports normal body functions. If you notice persistent changes in your energy or performance, talk to a healthcare provider or sports dietitian rather than assuming a deficiency.

Activity Before Performance Micronutrient Audit 0 min

  • Think about everything you consume 1–3 hours before practice
  • Include foods, drinks, timing, and portion size
  • Identify whether your choices are mostly:
  • quick snacks (chips, candy, energy drinks)
  • balanced meals (carbs + protein + fruit/vegetable)
  • skipped fuel (nothing or very little)

Then answer:

  • “What is my body actually being given before I train?”
  • “Am I consistently giving my body enough variety to support vitamins and minerals?”

Step 2: Body Response Rating (How You Actually Feel)

Connect fueling directly to performance outcomes.

Rate your typical practice experience (1–10 scale) in four areas:

  • Energy: Do I feel strong early and steady late—or tired quickly?
  • Focus: Can I remember plays, react fast, and stay locked in?
  • Endurance: Do I fade, slow down, or finish strong?
  • Physical comfort: Do I experience cramps, dizziness, or heaviness?

Then complete:

  • “My body during practice usually feels…” (1–2 sentences describing patterns)

Step 3: Micronutrient Connection Analysis (What Might Be Missing?)

Connect your pre-practice habits to micronutrient support, not just calories.

Guiding prompts:

  • “What vitamins or minerals might I NOT be getting before practice?”
  • “Could this be affecting how my body performs?”

Identify at least one gap:

  • Low iron support → feeling tired, low stamina
  • Low calcium/vitamin D support → weaker muscle function, slower recovery
  • Low electrolytes (hydration) → cramps, headaches, fatigue
  • Low fruit/vegetable intake → poor recovery, low energy stability

Students complete:

  • “My biggest pre-practice gap is likely __________, and it may be affecting my __________ (energy, focus, endurance, recovery).”

Step 4: Pattern Recognition (Connecting the Dots)

Look for repeated trends instead of one-day answers.

Then answer:

  • “On my best practices, what did I eat beforehand?”
  • “On my worst practices, what did I eat beforehand?”
  • “What pattern do I notice between food quality and performance?”

Then they write:

  • “My performance improves when I __________________________________.”
  • “My performance drops when I __________________________________.”

Step 5: Upgrade Planning (What I Will Change)

Choose one improvement category:

  • Add a fruit or vegetable (micronutrient boost)
  • Add a calcium-rich food (muscle and bone support)
  • Add hydration with electrolytes (cramp prevention and endurance)
  • Replace low-nutrient snack with balanced fuel (energy stability)

Design an upgraded pre-practice option:

  • “A better pre-practice fuel for me would be __________________ because it supports my __________________.”

Commitment Step: Performance Rule Creation

Create a clear, repeatable rule tied directly to performance outcomes.

Sentence frame:

  • “Before practice, I will eat/drink __________ at __________ time to improve my __________ (energy, focus, endurance) and reduce my risk of __________ (fatigue, cramps, poor recovery).”

1. A student eats only chips and a sports drink 1 hour before practice and feels tired halfway through warm-ups. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is receiving steady iron for endurance
  • B. Their body is missing key vitamins and minerals that support energy stability Correct
  • C. Their muscles are growing too quickly from excess sodium
  • D. Their hydration levels are perfect for performance

2. A student skips breakfast and feels lightheaded and unfocused during morning practice. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their muscles are over-recovered from sleep
  • B. Their vitamin intake is too high
  • C. Their body lacks nutrients needed for energy and focus Correct
  • D. Their protein intake is too high

3. A student drinks plenty of water but rarely eats fruits or vegetables and notices slow recovery. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein intake is too low
  • B. Their body is missing vitamins that support recovery and energy regulation Correct
  • C. Their hydration is too high and causing fatigue
  • D. Their sodium levels are too high

4. A student eats a banana before practice and feels steady energy instead of an early crash. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their muscles are not using energy properly
  • B. Their body is using quick-digesting nutrients to support energy and focus Correct
  • C. Their hydration is too low
  • D. Their body is storing excess fat immediately

5. A student frequently gets muscle cramps and rarely consumes electrolyte-rich fluids. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their iron levels are too high
  • B. Their protein intake is too high
  • C. Their body lacks minerals needed for muscle and nerve function Correct
  • D. Their bones are growing too quickly

6. A student eats a balanced snack before practice and reports improved focus and endurance. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their hydration is unnecessary
  • B. Their body is overloaded with sugar
  • C. Their body is receiving nutrients that support stable energy and performance Correct

7. A student feels unusually tired during practice despite sleeping well but often skips meals. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their muscles are overdeveloped
  • B. Their vitamin intake is too high

  • A. Their protein intake is too high
  • B. Their body is missing a variety of micronutrients needed for brain and body function Correct

  • C. Their hydration is decreasing
  • D. Their vitamin intake is too high

11. A student feels dizzy and sluggish after not eating or drinking much all morning. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein intake is too high
  • B. Their muscles are too strong for movement
  • C. Their body is low on fuel and fluids needed for performance Correct
  • D. Their bones are overdeveloped

12. A student eats colorful fruits and vegetables daily and notices better recovery after workouts. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their hydration is unnecessary
  • B. Their protein is being wasted
  • C. Their body is receiving vitamins that support recovery and energy balance Correct
  • D. Their muscles are not adapting

13. A student eats nothing before afternoon practice and struggles with reaction time and decision-making. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their muscles are overtrained
  • B. Their protein intake is too high
  • C. Their body lacks fuel needed for focus and performance Correct
  • D. Their hydration is too strong

14. A student starts eating a small balanced meal before practice and notices fewer cramps and better endurance. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is losing strength
  • B. Their hydration is too low
  • C. Their body is receiving electrolytes and nutrients that support muscle function Correct
  • D. Their protein is no longer needed

15. A student notices their worst practices always follow days of poor food variety and skipped meals. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their sleep is the only factor affecting performance
  • B. Their body performance is strongly influenced by consistent micronutrient intake Correct
  • C. Their muscles are permanently weak
  • D. Their hydration has no effect on performance
