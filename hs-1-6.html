Macronutrients and Physical Activity

High School Course 1, Lesson 6

Video Macronutrients and Physical Activity 6 min

Reading Macronutrients and Physical Activity 10 min

What you eat before, during, and after physical activity can influence how you perform and recover. This lesson covers general fueling principles organized by activity duration, intensity, and time available before exercise.

Important note: Athletes with medical conditions or high training loads should follow guidance from a sports dietitian or medical team. These are general principles, not personalized prescriptions.

Carbohydrates: Your Quick Energy Powerhouse

What They Do for Your Body

Carbohydrates are your body's primary and preferred source of energy. When you eat carbs, your body breaks them down into glucose, which gets stored as glycogen in your liver and muscles. This is your immediate energy reserve—the fuel your body reaches for first when you need a quick burst of power.

Why This Matters to You

As exercise intensity increases, your body primarily burns carbohydrates. This means carbs are what fuel your sprint to the finish line, your explosive jump, or your ability to push hard during the final minutes of competition. Without enough carbs, you lose that quick energy, and your athletic performance takes a hit. You'll feel that sudden energy crash—the "wall" athletes talk about—that makes it impossible to give your best effort.

When to Eat Them

Consume simple carbohydrates before and during intense activities or workouts lasting longer than 60 minutes. This keeps your energy levels stable and your performance sharp throughout.

What Happens When You Don't Eat Enough Carbs

  • Sudden energy crash: You hit the wall and feel completely drained
  • Heavy legs: Your muscles feel sluggish and slow to respond, making it harder to move explosively
  • Brain fog: Your focus deteriorates, making it difficult to concentrate during competition or training
  • Dizziness: Low blood sugar can make you feel lightheaded or uncoordinated
  • Poor athletic performance: You can't push as hard or perform at your best

Protein: The Recovery and Strength Builder

What It Does for Your Body

Protein provides the components your body needs to rebuild muscle and tissue, helping your body adapt to the stress of exercise. During any workout, you create tiny microtears in your muscle fibers. Protein is what repairs these tears and builds stronger muscle in their place.

Why This Matters to You

This is where your strength gains come from. Without adequate protein after exercise, your body can't rebuild muscle effectively, which means you won't get stronger, faster, or more powerful. Instead, your body shifts into a catabolic state—meaning it actually breaks down muscle tissue rather than building it up. This directly impacts your athletic performance, your appearance, and your ability to progress in your sport or fitness goals.

When to Eat It

Consume protein after physical activity, paired with carbohydrates. This combination helps your body recover faster and replaces the energy stores you used during exercise. The sooner you eat after working out, the sooner your muscles can start rebuilding.

What Happens When You Don't Eat Enough Protein

  • Increased and prolonged muscle soreness: Recovery takes longer, and you feel sore for days
  • Loss of strength and endurance: Your athletic performance plateaus or declines because your muscles aren't being rebuilt stronger
  • Decreased coordination: Without proper muscle recovery, your body can't perform complex movements as effectively
  • No visible progress: You won't see the physical changes or strength gains you're working hard to achieve

Fats: Your Endurance and Recovery Fuel

What They Do for Your Body

Fats are a concentrated source of energy that's especially important for sustaining moderate-intensity or endurance activities. They're the primary fuel source during low-energy, low-intensity activities. Once your glycogen stores run low after a long period of exercise, your body switches to burning fat for sustained energy.

Why This Matters to You

Fats provide long-lasting energy, which is crucial if you're doing cardio, distance running, or any activity that requires sustained effort over time. They also play a critical role in hormone balance—when fat intake is too low, your hormones become imbalanced, which affects your mood, energy levels, and recovery speed. Plus, fats help reduce inflammation after intense workouts, which means faster recovery and less prolonged soreness.

When to Eat Them

Consume fats 2–3 hours before or after physical activity. Fats digest slowly, so eating them right before or during exercise can cause digestive discomfort and actually hurt your performance. However, during recovery hours after activity, your body needs fats to reduce inflammation and support hormone balance.

What Happens When You Don't Eat Enough Fats

  • Early fatigue during endurance activities: You run out of sustained energy and hit a wall
  • Poor exercise performance: Your body struggles to meet the demands of prolonged activity
  • Delayed recovery: Inflammation lingers longer, and you feel sore and stiff for days
  • Hormone imbalances: Your mood suffers, your energy becomes inconsistent, and your body can't regulate stress hormones effectively
  • Difficulty concentrating: Hormone imbalances directly impact focus and mental clarity

Hydration and heat safety: Staying hydrated before, during, and after physical activity is essential. Drink water regularly throughout the day, especially around exercise. In hot or humid conditions, pay extra attention to hydration. Signs of dehydration include dizziness, dry mouth, dark urine, and feeling unusually tired. If you experience these, rest and rehydrate. Athletes with medical conditions should follow their sports dietitian's or medical team's guidance for individualized hydration and fueling plans.

  • Specific (not "eat better"—but "eat a banana 30 minutes before practice")

  • Measurable (you'll know if you did it)
