The Truth About Micronutrients

Ever wonder why some days you feel like you could run a marathon, and other days you can barely stay awake in class? Or why your skin breaks out right before an important event? Or why you crush it at practice one week and feel sluggish the next?

The answer might not be what you think. It's not always about sleep or stress. Often, it comes down to micronutrients—the tiny vitamins and minerals your body absolutely needs to function. Without them, everything from your energy levels to your mood to your athletic performance falls apart.

Here's the deal: while macronutrients (like carbs, protein, and fat) give your body fuel, micronutrients are the workers behind the scenes. They're the chemical regulators that help your body produce the enzymes and hormones that keep you sharp, energized, and feeling your best.

What Are Micronutrients?

Micronutrients are essential vitamins and minerals your body needs in small amounts. Think of them as the difference between having a phone (macronutrients) and having the right apps and software to actually make it work (micronutrients).

There are two main types: vitamins and minerals. Let's break down what each one does for you.

Vitamins: The Organic Powerhouses

What they are: Vitamins are organic compounds made by plants and animals. Your body can't make most of them on its own, so you have to get them from food.

Why this matters to you: Without vitamins, your body can't repair the damage that happens every day from stress, exercise, and just living. You literally can't heal properly without them.

The Two Types of Vitamins

Fat-Soluble Vitamins (Vitamins A, D, E, K)

These vitamins dissolve in fat and stick around in your body longer. This means you can build them up over time, but you also need to be careful not to get too much.

  • Vitamin A supports normal vision and immune function
  • Vitamin D helps your bones stay strong (important if you're athletic or want to avoid injuries) and actually affects your mood—lack of it can make you feel depressed or unmotivated
  • Vitamin E acts as an antioxidant and supports normal cell function
  • Vitamin K helps your blood clot, so if you get a cut or injury, you heal faster

Water-Soluble Vitamins (Vitamin C and B-Complex Vitamins)

These dissolve in water and don't stick around in your body as long, so you need them more regularly.

  • Vitamin C supports normal immune function and tissue repair
  • B-Complex Vitamins support normal energy metabolism and nervous system function

What Vitamins Do in Your Body

Vitamins work hard to:

  • Repair cellular damage from exercise, stress, and environmental factors (meaning you recover faster from workouts and bounce back from tough weeks)
  • Regulate hormones that control your mood, energy, and how you feel overall
  • Release energy from food so you actually feel energized instead of sluggish after eating
  • Keep nerves, immune system, and organs functioning properly so you can focus, stay healthy, and perform at your best

When Vitamin Intake Goes Wrong

Vitamin Deficiency: When the body does not get enough of a vitamin over time, it can affect normal body functions. Symptoms like fatigue or brain fog have many possible causes and should not be self-diagnosed. If you have persistent health concerns, talk to a healthcare provider.

Hypervitaminosis (Vitamin Toxicity): When you get too much of a vitamin (usually from supplements), it can cause problems like headaches, nausea, or nerve damage.

The Balance: That's where Recommended Daily Allowances (RDA), Adequate Intake (AI), and Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (UL) come in. These guidelines, based on your age and gender, tell you how much of each vitamin you actually need—not too little, not too much.

Minerals: The Building Blocks

What they are: Minerals are inorganic substances found in water and soil. Your body absorbs them through plants and animals (which is why eating a variety of foods matters).

Why this matters to you: Minerals literally build your body—your bones, teeth, and muscles. Without them, you're weak, your bones are fragile, and your body can't do basic things like regulate your heartbeat or send signals from your brain to your muscles.

The Two Types of Minerals

Macrominerals (the body needs these in larger amounts)

These include calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium. They're the main structural components of your body.

  • Calcium builds strong bones and teeth (especially important now while your skeleton is still developing) and helps your muscles contract during exercise
  • Magnesium supports normal muscle and nerve function
  • Potassium regulates your heartbeat and helps muscles contract, so you perform better athletically

Trace Minerals (the body needs these in smaller amounts)

These include iron, zinc, copper, and iodine. Even though you need less of them, they're just as critical.

  • Iron carries oxygen to your muscles and brain—without it, you feel tired and can't concentrate in class
  • Zinc supports your immune system (fewer infections) and helps your skin heal faster from acne or cuts

What Minerals Do in Your Body

Minerals work to:

  • Build and maintain tissues like bones, muscles, and connective tissue (stronger bones = fewer injuries, more power in athletics)
  • Regulate fluid balance and nerve signals so your brain can communicate with your body and your muscles respond when you need them to
  • Support the immune system so you stay healthy and don't miss school or activities
  • Trigger the chemical reactions your body uses to maintain metabolism and produce energy (this is why mineral deficiency makes you feel exhausted)

When Mineral Intake Goes Wrong

Mineral Deficiency: Inadequate mineral intake over time can affect normal body functions. However, symptoms like fatigue, weakness, or poor focus have many possible causes and should be assessed by a healthcare professional rather than self-diagnosed.

Mineral Toxicity (Mineral Overload): Getting too much of a mineral (usually from supplements) can cause nausea, weakness, or organ damage.

The Balance: Just like with vitamins, RDA, AI, and UL guidelines help you get the right amount of each mineral for your age and gender.

The Bottom Line

Your body is constantly working, and micronutrients are the difference between feeling amazing and feeling terrible. They're why some days you're sharp, focused, and energized—and why other days you're foggy, moody, and exhausted.

The good news? You have control. By eating a variety of whole foods—fruits, vegetables, lean proteins, whole grains, and dairy—you can get most of the micronutrients your body needs. And when you do, you'll notice the difference: better focus in class, more energy at practice, clearer skin, better mood, and faster recovery.

It's not just about health. It's about being your best self.

Activity Your Micronutrient Patterns 45 min

Rate how you function in each of these areas on a scale of 1 to 10. 1 is the lowest and 10 is the highest.

  • Energy level
  • Focus in class
  • Athletic performance
  • Sleep quality
  • Mood

Part 2: Identify Patterns

Examine their typical weekly eating habits and identify:

Fruit intake:

  • 0–1 days per week
  • 2–3 days per week
  • 4–5 days per week
  • 6–7 days per week

Vegetable intake

  • 0–1 days per week
  • 2–3 days per week
  • 4–5 days per week
  • 6–7 days per week

Dairy or calcium-rich foods:

  • 0–1 days per week
  • 2–3 days per week
  • 4–5 days per week
  • 6–7 days per week

Whole grain intake:

  • 0–1 days per week
  • 2–3 days per week
  • 4–5 days per week
  • 6–7 days per week

Protein variety:

  • Mostly the same protein source every day
  • Two different protein sources during the week
  • Three to four different protein sources during the week
  • Five or more different protein sources during the week

Part 3: Reflection Discussion

  • Which category is your strongest habit right now?
  • Which category is your weakest habit right now?
  • How do you usually feel on days when you eat more fruits and vegetables?
  • How do you usually feel on days when you eat fewer nutrient-rich foods?
  • Could micronutrient-rich foods help improve your energy, focus, mood, or athletic performance if you increased it?
