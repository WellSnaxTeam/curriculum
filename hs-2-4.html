Key Minerals

High School Course 2, Lesson 4

Video Key Minerals 12 min

Reading Key Minerals 15 min

Minerals are essential nutrients that play many important roles in your body — from building bones to supporting nerve and muscle function. Your body needs a variety of minerals from food, and each one has specific functions. This lesson covers the key minerals and their normal roles in the body.

Important note: Many factors affect how you feel day to day — including sleep, stress, hydration, physical activity, and overall dietary patterns. While minerals play important supporting roles, symptoms like fatigue, cramps, or poor focus can have many causes and should not be self-diagnosed as mineral deficiencies. If you're concerned about symptoms, talk to a healthcare provider.

Let's break down which minerals matter and their normal functions.

Macrominerals:

They are the minerals that your body requires the greatest amount of.

Calcium

Why you need it:

  • Builds and maintains bones and teeth
  • Needed for muscle contraction and nerve signaling
  • Helps blood clot

Where to get it:

Dairy products, dark leafy greens, fish, almonds

Phosphorus

Why you need it:

  • Works with calcium to build bones and teeth
  • Part of cell membranes, DNA and ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy

Where to get it:

Meat, poultry, dairy, fish, nuts, seeds, legumes, cereal

Magnesium

Why you need it:

  • Supports normal muscle contraction and nerve function
  • Plays a role in enzyme reactions involved in energy metabolism
  • Contributes to normal blood sugar regulation as part of overall metabolism

Where to get it:

Seeds, nuts, legumes, dark leafy greens, dark chocolate, whole grains, meat

Sodium

Why you need it:

  • Helps maintain fluid balance
  • Needed for nerve impulses and muscle contraction
  • Most people in the United States get more than they need. It is lost in sweat, so students who sweat heavily during long or hot activity may need to replace more

Where to get it:

Table salt, condiments and sauces, grain products, processed meats, restaurant foods

(Note: Most people get plenty of sodium from everyday foods — sometimes too much. However, individual sodium needs vary based on activity level, sweat rate, and medical conditions. Athletes who sweat heavily may have different needs. General sodium guidance should not replace personalized advice from a healthcare provider or sports dietitian.)

Potassium

Why you need it:

  • Works with sodium to maintain fluid balance
  • Supports normal heart rhythm, nerve signaling and muscle contraction

Where to get it:

Potatoes and yams, tomatoes, avocados, melons, bananas, beans, lentils, nuts

Chloride

Why you need it:

  • Helps maintain fluid and pH balance
  • Part of stomach acid, which helps digest food
  • Helps red blood cells carry carbon dioxide out of the body

Where to get it:

Table salt, seafood and meats, dairy, seaweed, vegetables, whole grains

Sulfur

Why you need it:

  • Part of some amino acids, so it is found in proteins throughout the body, including muscle, skin and connective tissue
  • Most people get enough from protein foods

Where to get it:

Animal proteins, dairy, cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, brussels sprouts), allium vegetables (garlic, onion, shallots), legumes and nuts

Trace Minerals:

Your body requires these in smaller amounts.

Iron

Two types:

  • Heme iron → absorbed easily from animal sources
  • Non-heme iron → from plant sources (pair with vitamin C for better absorption)

Why you need it:

  • Part of hemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, and myoglobin, which holds oxygen in muscle
  • Needed for enzymes that help the body use energy
  • Supports normal immune function

Where to get it:

Clams, chicken, oysters, beef, pumpkin seeds, soybeans, lentils

Zinc

Why you need it:

  • Needed for many enzyme reactions
  • Supports normal immune function and wound healing
  • Supports growth and taste

Where to get it:

Oysters, red meat, seafood, poultry, dairy, eggs, legumes, nuts

Iodine

Why you need it:

  • Needed to make thyroid hormones, which help regulate metabolism
  • Supports normal growth and nervous system development

Where to get it:

Iodized table salt, seaweed, cod, shellfish, dairy, eggs

Fluoride

Why you need it:

  • Helps make tooth enamel more resistant to acid and helps prevent cavities
  • Also found in bones

Where to get it:

Brewed black and green tea, grape juice, canned seafood and shellfish, raisins, potatoes

Selenium

Why you need it:

  • Needed for normal thyroid hormone function
  • Part of enzymes that help protect cells from damage
  • Supports normal immune function

Where to get it:

Brazil nuts, seafood, meat and poultry, dairy, eggs

Copper

Why you need it:

  • Helps the body use iron and make red blood cells
  • Needed for enzymes involved in energy production, connective tissue and the nervous system

Where to get it:

Organ meats, shellfish, nuts and seeds, potatoes, grains

Manganese

Why you need it:

  • Part of enzymes involved in metabolism and protecting cells from damage
  • Helps form bone and cartilage

Where to get it:

Seafood, whole grains, nuts and seeds, legumes, teas and spices

Chromium

Why you need it:

  • May help insulin work normally
  • Involved in how the body processes carbohydrate, fat and protein

Where to get it:

Grape juice, beef, orange juice, apples, green beans, bananas

Molybdenum

Why you need it:

  • Part of enzymes that break down sulfites and certain amino acids
  • Helps the body process some waste products

Where to get it:

Legumes, dairy, organ meats, grains, potatoes, bananas, leafy greens

The Bottom Line

Getting a variety of minerals from food supports many normal body functions — including bone health, nerve signaling, muscle contraction, and energy metabolism. The best approach is to eat a balanced diet with a variety of whole foods — fruits, vegetables, dairy or alternatives, lean proteins, nuts, seeds, and whole grains.

Cramps, fatigue, or performance issues can have many causes. While mineral intake can be one factor, individualized electrolyte or supplement plans should come from a qualified healthcare provider or registered dietitian — especially for athletes or anyone with specific health concerns.

Focus on food sources first, and seek professional guidance for supplements.

Activity Mineral Case Studies 45 min

Review the fictional scenarios provided by your teacher and analyze how minerals relate to each situation.

  • Read each fictional student's described eating pattern
  • Identify which minerals might be relevant based on the foods described
  • Consider how their eating pattern relates to the symptoms described

After:

  • write a reflection describing how a varied food pattern supports normal body functions (instead of linking specific symptoms to specific mineral gaps)
  • Identify which minerals are most relevant for each fictional case
  • Suggest one realistic change for each fictional student

Part 2: Group Mineral Challenge

You will:

  • Using the teacher-provided fictional meal examples, identify which mineral groups are well-represented and which could be improved
  • Choose one meal to improve and explain your reasoning

Then:

  • brainstorm 2–3 realistic upgrades (not replacements, just improvements)
  • select one upgrade they can actually do this week
  • write a step-by-step plan:
  • “When will I do this?”
  • “What will I add or adjust?”
  • “How will I know it worked?”

Finish by:

  • identifying which minerals the meal plan provides well, and one food that could add a mineral it is low in
