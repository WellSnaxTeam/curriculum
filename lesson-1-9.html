Food Marketing and Claims

Course 1, Lesson 9

Video Watch: Video: Food Marketing and Claims 11 min

Reading How to Read Food Marketing: Spotting Truth, Half-Truths, and False Claims 15-20 min

Why this matters: Food companies use words, pictures, and labels to get you to notice their products. Learning how to read marketing helps you make healthier choices.

What is food marketing? Any way a company promotes a food or drink — package labels, ads, in-store displays, celebrity endorsements.

Types of claims — different rules apply:

  • Nutrient-content claims (regulated by FDA): "Good source of fiber," "Low fat," "High in protein" — these have specific legal definitions. "High in" means 20% DV or more; "Good source" means 10–19% DV.
  • Health claims (regulated by FDA): "A diet rich in calcium may reduce the risk of osteoporosis" — these must be backed by scientific evidence and approved wording.
  • Structure/function claims (not FDA-reviewed): "Supports immune health," "Helps maintain energy" — these describe how a nutrient affects the body but do not need FDA pre-approval.
  • Unregulated marketing terms: "Natural," "Wholesome," "Artisan," "Made with real fruit" — these have no legal definition and may be used loosely. Always check the ingredient list and Nutrition Facts to judge the real story.

How to verify any front-of-package claim: Compare it against the serving size, Nutrition Facts (especially added sugars, fiber, saturated fat, sodium), and the ingredient list. A claim on the front only tells part of the picture.

Activity Claims Analysis 20 min

Evaluate three packages of foods to determine if you can trust the food. Pick three packaged foods you often see (cereal, snack bar, drink, etc.). For each, write:

1. The main claim on the front.

2. Two things the claim might be trying to distract you from (e.g., high sugar).

3. One number from the Nutrition Facts that helps you judge the claim.

4. Your conclusion: Trust it / Be skeptical / Don't trust it — with one sentence why.

Quiz Food Marketing Quiz 15 min

Passing score: 70%

1. Which statement best describes a common advertising technique used to make a food product seem healthier than it is?

  • A. Listing the exact serving size on the Nutrition Facts label
  • B. Using phrases like "made with real fruit" while adding large amounts of sugar Correct
  • C. Providing the manufacturer's contact information on the package
  • D. Including the number of calories per serving

2. A cereal box claims "whole grain" in big letters, but the ingredient list shows "enriched flour" as the first ingredient. What should a student conclude?

  • A. The cereal is definitely high in fiber and low in sugar
  • B. The front claim may be misleading because ingredients are listed by weight Correct
  • C. Enriched flour is another name for whole grain, so the claim is accurate
  • D. The Nutrition Facts label is not required when ingredients are listed

3. Which part of the Nutrition Facts label tells you how much of a vitamin or mineral is in a serving relative to daily needs?

  • A. Ingredient list
  • B. Percent Daily Value (%DV) Correct
  • C. Serving size statement
  • D. Product name

4. A snack package says "low fat" but one serving contains 300 calories and 35 grams of sugar. Which most accurately describes this product?

  • A. Low fat means healthy, so it's a good choice for every diet
  • B. The claim focuses on one nutrient; other nutrients (like sugar and calories) may make it an unhealthy choice Correct
  • C. Low fat automatically means low calorie and low sugar
  • D. If a product is labeled low fat, the ingredient list is irrelevant

5. A soda company uses cartoon characters and bright colors on its packaging aimed at children. Which concept from consumer health does this best illustrate?

  • A. Food safety rules
  • B. Advertising techniques that target children Correct
  • C. How to read the Nutrition Facts label
  • D. The role of vitamins and minerals

6. When comparing two yogurts, one lists "natural flavors" and the other lists specific ingredients (milk, fruit, sugar). Why is the second label more helpful?

  • A. "Natural flavors" always means the product is unhealthy
  • B. Specific ingredient lists give clearer information about added sugars and real ingredients Correct
  • C. Products that list "natural flavors" have no calories
  • D. Ingredient lists are not required to be accurate

7. A company advertises its chips as "baked, not fried." Which critical question should a student ask next?

  • A. Does the package include a picture of a person enjoying the chips?
  • B. What is the serving size, and how many calories, fat, and sodium are in one serving? Correct
  • C. Is the product made by a local company?
  • D. Does the product include a coupon on the box?

8. Which of the following is a reliable sign that a health claim on a food package may be exaggerated or misleading?

  • A. The package includes a Nutrition Facts label and ingredient list
  • B. The claim uses vague words like "natural," "wholesome," or "healthy" without supporting details Correct
  • C. The product lists allergens clearly on the label
  • D. The serving size is listed in common household measures

9. A student wants to choose a snack that provides lasting energy for after-school activities. Which label information is most useful?

  • A. The color and design of the package
  • B. The grams of protein and fiber per serving and the total calories per serving Correct
  • C. Whether the product uses celebrity endorsements
  • D. The number of images of fruit on the front of the package

10. Which consumer right helps a person check whether a food company's health claim is accurate?

  • A. The right to be heard
  • B. The right to consumer education (access to accurate information) Correct
  • C. The right to have problems corrected only after purchase
  • D. The right to enjoy unlimited samples
