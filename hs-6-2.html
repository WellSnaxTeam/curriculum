Underfueling and Energy Deficiency

High School Course 6, Lesson 2

Video Underfueling and Energy Deficiency 5 min

Reading Why Fueling Matters 10 min

Have you ever walked into practice feeling confident, only to find yourself tired halfway through? Maybe your legs felt heavy, you couldn't focus on your coach, or you became frustrated over mistakes you normally wouldn't make. Sometimes athletes think they just had a "bad day," but often the real problem started long before practice—with what they did (or didn't) eat.

Your body is like a car. Even the fastest sports car cannot go very far without fuel. Your muscles and brain work the same way. Before physical activity, your body needs energy from food to help you move, think, react, and recover. When you don't eat enough—or you eat foods that don't provide lasting energy—you may experience underfueling.

What Is Underfueling?

Underfueling happens when your body does not receive enough energy (calories) or nutrients to meet the demands of your daily activities and exercise. This can happen if you skip meals, avoid certain foods, wait too long to eat before activity, or simply don't eat enough throughout the day.

When your body doesn't have enough fuel, it has to work harder to perform the same tasks. Instead of feeling strong and energized, you may feel like you're running on empty.

How Underfueling Affects You

Underfueling doesn't just affect your workout—it affects your entire day.

  • Energy: You tire more quickly and may struggle to finish practices or games.
  • Focus: Your brain depends on glucose from carbohydrates. Without enough fuel, it becomes harder to pay attention, make quick decisions, and remember plays.
  • Mood: Low energy can make you feel irritable, frustrated, or unmotivated.
  • Athletic Performance: Your speed, strength, endurance, coordination, and reaction time can all decrease.
  • Appearance and Recovery: Over time, underfueling may slow muscle recovery, increase injury risk, and make it harder to maintain healthy hair, skin, and muscle.

What Are Energy Deficiencies?

An energy deficiency happens when your body consistently uses more energy than it receives from food. While one skipped meal may leave you tired for a few hours, repeated underfueling can create a long-term energy deficiency.

Your body begins to conserve energy by slowing down important functions. Instead of using energy for athletic performance and muscle recovery, it focuses on keeping your heart, lungs, and other organs working.

This means you may notice:

  • Feeling tired even after sleeping
  • Declining athletic performance
  • Difficulty concentrating in class
  • Slower recovery after workouts
  • Frequent soreness
  • Increased illness because your immune system is weaker
  • Feeling cold more often
  • Increased hunger or cravings later in the day

These are signs that your body is asking for more fuel.

Common Symptoms of Underfueling

Everyone experiences underfueling differently, but common signs include:

  • Low energy during practice
  • Feeling dizzy or lightheaded
  • Headaches
  • Shakiness
  • Trouble concentrating
  • Mood swings or irritability
  • Weakness
  • Early muscle fatigue
  • Slower running speeds or lower strength
  • Needing longer to recover after exercise

Ignoring these symptoms can make practices feel harder than they should and reduce your ability to improve.

The Role of Macronutrients

The foods you eat before activity matter just as much as eating enough. Your body depends on three major nutrients called macronutrients.

Carbohydrates: Your Primary Fuel

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred source of energy during exercise. They are broken down into glucose, which powers both your muscles and your brain.

Good carbohydrate choices include:

  • Fruit
  • Oatmeal
  • Whole-grain bread
  • Rice
  • Pasta
  • Potatoes
  • Whole-grain cereal

Eating carbohydrates before activity helps you:

  • Maintain energy throughout practice
  • Stay mentally focused
  • Delay fatigue
  • Improve endurance and speed

Protein: Helps You Recover

Protein is not your main fuel during exercise, but it helps repair and build muscles after activity.

Healthy protein choices include:

  • Greek yogurt
  • Eggs
  • Lean chicken or turkey
  • Peanut butter
  • Beans
  • Cottage cheese
  • Milk

Including some protein before activity can help you stay satisfied and begin supporting muscle recovery.

Healthy Fats: Long-Lasting Energy

Healthy fats provide long-lasting energy throughout the day, although they digest more slowly than carbohydrates.

Healthy fat sources include:

  • Nuts
  • Seeds
  • Peanut butter
  • Avocados

Large amounts of high-fat foods immediately before intense exercise may leave you feeling sluggish because they take longer to digest.

What Should You Eat Before Activity?

A good pre-activity meal combines carbohydrates with a moderate amount of protein.

Examples include:

  • Oatmeal with fruit
  • Peanut butter on whole-grain toast
  • Greek yogurt with berries
  • Turkey sandwich on whole-grain bread
  • Banana with peanut butter
  • Trail mix with dried fruit and nuts

If you have less than an hour before activity, choose a lighter snack that is mostly carbohydrates, such as fruit, applesauce, or whole-grain crackers.

Healthy Habits That Keep You Fueled

Good fueling is not just about one meal before practice. It is built through daily habits.

Try these habits:

  • Eat breakfast every day.
  • Avoid skipping meals.
  • Eat every 3–4 hours during the day.
  • Include carbohydrates in meals and snacks.
  • Add protein to help with recovery.
  • Drink water throughout the day instead of waiting until practice.
  • Plan ahead by packing snacks when you know you'll be active after school.
  • Try new pre-practice meals during regular practices instead of on game day.

Small habits repeated every day lead to better performance when it matters most.

Putting It All Together

Your body performs best when it has enough fuel. Underfueling can lower your energy, hurt your mood, reduce your focus, slow your recovery, and keep you from performing at your best. By eating enough throughout the day and choosing meals that include carbohydrates, protein, and healthy fats, you give your body what it needs to train hard, recover well, and continue improving.

The next time you step onto the field, court, track, or weight room, remember this: your performance doesn't just begin when practice starts—it begins with how you fuel your body beforehand.

If you are concerned that you may be struggling with underfueling, reach out to a trusted adult or doctor to ensure your safety and health.
