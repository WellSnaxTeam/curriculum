What are macronutrients?

High School Course 1, Lesson 1

Reading What are Macronutrients? 5 min

Close your eyes for a second. How do you feel at this exact moment? Energized? Sluggish? Focused? Scattered?

Here's the thing: what you ate today is literally the reason. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Today. Your breakfast, your snack, that energy drink—they're all affecting how your brain works, how fast you can move, and how you handle stress right now.

The nutrients you eat are your body's operating system. And we're going to show you exactly how.

What Are Macronutrients?

Macronutrients are the essential nutrients your body needs in large amounts to function. Think of them as the three main fuel sources that power everything you do—from thinking in class to recovering from workouts.

The three macronutrients are:

  • Carbohydrates — the primary source of fuel
  • Protein — responsible for all of the work your cells perform
  • Fats — helps the body absorb vitamins

How your habits affect your results

Here's the real connection: what you choose to eat directly impacts how you feel and perform—both today and for years to come.

How Each Macronutrient Works in Your Body

Carbohydrates

  • The body's primary source of fuel
  • Regulates digestion
  • Regulates blood sugar levels

Protein

  • Builds and repairs tissue
  • Aids the immune system
  • Regulates hormones

Fats

  • Aid in nutrient absorption
  • Provides insulation
  • Responsible for healthy brain and nerve function

ATHLETES: This Matters Even MORE for You

If you play sports or work out regularly, macronutrients aren't just about health—they're about performance and recovery.

  • Carbs fuel your workouts — Without enough, you'll feel weak and tired during practice.
  • Protein rebuilds muscle — After you work out, protein repairs the tiny tears in your muscles, making them stronger.
  • Fats support your brain — Better focus = better decisions on the field or court.

Real talk: Athletes who eat strategically perform better, recover faster, and stay healthier. What you eat is part of your training.

The Bottom Line

Without these nutrients, your body goes into "preservation mode"—organ systems slow down, and your body starts breaking down its own tissue for energy. That's not just uncomfortable; it's dangerous.

You get to choose every single meal, whether you're fueling up or breaking down. Now you know what each macronutrient does. What will you choose today?

Quiz Macronutrients Quiz 25 min

Passing score: 70%

1. A student eats a large bowl of plain pasta about 1 hour before soccer practice and notices they can sprint repeatedly during the first half of practice. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is using stored fat for quick bursts of energy.
  • B. Their carbohydrate intake increased available blood glucose and muscle glycogen for high-intensity activity. Correct
  • C. Their protein is being used as the primary energy source for sprints.
  • D. Their vitamin stores are fueling immediate muscle contractions.

2. A student skips breakfast and feels lightheaded and unable to concentrate during a morning chemistry test. Which macronutrient-related cause is most likely?

  • A. Low protein causing immediate loss of neurotransmitters.
  • B. Dehydration from lack of water intake only.
  • C. Low available carbohydrates resulting in reduced blood glucose for brain function. Correct
  • D. Excess fat slowing digestion and causing fatigue.

3. A student eats a snack with nuts and cheese between weightlifting sets and notices they feel full and have more stable energy over the next hour. What macronutrient effect best explains this?

  • A. Fats and proteins slow gastric emptying, maintaining steady energy and satiety. Correct
  • B. Carbohydrates from the snack rapidly spike blood sugar then crash.
  • C. The snack increases fiber absorption that immediately raises energy.
  • D. Micronutrients in nuts cause instant muscle recovery.

4. During a long-distance run, a student "hits the wall" (sudden extreme fatigue) after about 90 minutes. Which explanation related to macronutrients is most likely?

  • A. Rapid depletion of stored muscle glycogen (carbohydrates). Correct
  • B. Lack of consumed protein during the race.
  • C. Excess dietary fat blocking oxygen delivery.
  • D. Too much water diluting blood nutrients.

5. A student follows a very low-carbohydrate diet for a week before a timed mile and notices they feel slower and breathe heavier. What macronutrient-related mechanism likely caused this change?

  • A. Increased protein turned into glucose instantly, improving performance.
  • B. Reduced carbohydrate availability forced greater reliance on fat oxidation, which is slower for high-intensity efforts. Correct
  • C. Higher fat intake directly increases oxygen transport efficiency.
  • D. Lower fat intake causes reduced ATP production at rest.

6. A student drinks a protein shake immediately after a resistance-training session and later reports less muscle soreness and quicker recovery. Which macronutrient role does this best illustrate?

  • A. Carbohydrates rebuild cell membranes.
  • B. Protein supplies amino acids necessary for muscle repair and synthesis. Correct
  • C. Fat converts to glycogen to replenish muscles.
  • D. Fiber increases protein absorption for recovery.

7. A student eating a high-sugar sports drink during a long practice performs well early but crashes with reduced focus later. Which combination best explains the pattern?

  • A. Rapid carbohydrate absorption gives quick energy, then a reactive hypoglycemia-like drop in blood glucose. Correct
  • B. Fat content in the drink prevented absorption of sugars.
  • C. Protein in the drink caused insulin suppression.
  • D. Vitamins in the drink caused overstimulation then fatigue.

8. A student follows a very high-protein, low-carbohydrate meal plan but complains of low endurance during gym class. Which is the most plausible macronutrient explanation?

  • A. Excess protein directly blocks oxygen uptake.
  • B. Insufficient carbohydrates reduce glycogen stores needed for sustained aerobic and anaerobic activity. Correct
  • C. Low fat causes immediate muscle cramps.
  • D. Protein transforms into fat quickly, limiting energy availability.

9. A student eats a balanced meal with whole grains, lean meat, and vegetables three hours before an exam and reports steady concentration. Which macronutrient principle supports this outcome?

  • A. Balanced meals combining complex carbohydrates, protein, and some fat provide sustained energy and stable blood glucose. Correct
  • B. Eating protein alone causes immediate cognitive boosts for hours.
  • C. High fat and no carbs will keep glucose stable for exams.
  • D. Micronutrients are the only factor affecting concentration.

10. True/False: A meal very high in fat and low in carbohydrates is ideal immediately before a 100-meter sprint because fat provides the fastest available energy for very short bursts.

  • A. True
  • B. False Correct

11. True/False: Consuming adequate carbohydrates in the days before a long athletic event helps "carb-load" and maximize muscle glycogen stores for improved endurance.

  • A. True Correct
  • B. False

12. A student drinks only water during a 3-hour basketball tournament and later experiences decreased performance and frequent cramps. Which macronutrient-related factor might contribute to cramps in addition to fluids and electrolytes?

  • A. Lack of carbohydrate intake leading to reduced muscle energy and possible early fatigue contributing to cramps. Correct
  • B. Excess protein intake during the tournament.
  • C. High fiber intake causing electrolyte imbalance.
  • D. Fat intake inhibits muscle contraction.

13. A cross-country runner eats a candy bar immediately before an interval workout and notices a burst of speed but then cannot sustain repeats. What best explains this response?

  • A. The candy bar's simple sugars caused a quick blood glucose rise and insulin response, giving short-term energy followed by a rapid drop. Correct
  • B. Candy is high in protein, which sustains long-term energy for intervals.
  • C. The fat in the candy bar provided immediate fuel for sprinting.
  • D. Fiber in the candy bar slowed digestion and sustained repeats.

14. A student regularly skips protein at dinner and finds they feel weaker in strength training the next day. Which macronutrient explanation is most likely?

  • A. Without evening protein, overnight muscle protein synthesis is reduced, limiting recovery and next-day strength. Correct
  • B. Lack of evening fat prevents glycogen storage.
  • C. Skipping protein increases carbohydrate storage, causing weakness.
  • D. Missing protein only affects flexibility, not strength.

15. A student uses an energy gel (high in simple carbohydrates) during a cycling race and maintains steady pace, but a teammate who uses the same gel without drinking water vomits. What is the most likely reason the teammate reacted poorly?

  • A. The teammate lacked the necessary enzymes to digest carbohydrates.
  • B. The concentrated carbohydrate gel without adequate water caused gastrointestinal distress; proper fluid intake helps gastric emptying and absorption. Correct
  • C. The gel contains protein that must be taken with fat to prevent vomiting.
  • D. The gel stimulated too much muscle glycogen synthesis causing nausea.
