Carbohydrates and Fuel Depletion

High School Course 6, Lesson 4

Video Carbohydrates and Fuel Depletion 3 min

Reading Inadequate Carbs and Fuel Depletion Effects on Performance 10 min

Picture this: You start practice feeling confident. During the warm-up, everything feels easy. But halfway through drills, your legs suddenly feel heavy. You struggle to keep up with your teammates. Your shots fall short, your sprint times slow down, and you can't seem to focus on what your coach is saying. By the end of practice, you're frustrated because you know you can perform better.

Many athletes think this happens because they aren't working hard enough or aren't in good enough shape. But sometimes the real problem starts long before practice begins—with what you ate (or didn't eat).

Your body needs fuel to move, think, react, and recover. One of the most important sources of that fuel is carbohydrates.

What Are Carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are one of the three main macronutrients your body needs, along with protein and fat. They are found in foods such as:

  • Bread
  • Rice
  • Pasta
  • Oatmeal
  • Potatoes
  • Fruit
  • Milk
  • Beans
  • Yogurt
  • Whole-grain cereals

Some carbohydrates digest quickly and provide fast energy, while others digest more slowly and provide energy over a longer period of time.

Every time you eat carbohydrates, you are giving your body the fuel it needs to stay active throughout the day.

Personal Connection: When you regularly eat enough healthy carbohydrates, you are more likely to feel energized during class, stay focused on schoolwork, and have enough energy left for practice or competition.

What Do Carbohydrates Do in the Body?

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred source of energy, especially during high-intensity and prolonged activity. However, individual needs vary — not every athlete requires the same amount of carbohydrates, and the body can also use fat for fuel at lower intensities.

After you eat carbohydrates, your digestive system breaks them down into glucose, a simple sugar that enters your bloodstream.

Your body uses glucose to:

  • Power your muscles during movement
  • Fuel your brain so you can think clearly
  • Support your nervous system
  • Keep your organs functioning properly

Any extra glucose your body doesn't immediately use is stored in your muscles and liver as glycogen.

Think of glycogen as your body's backup battery. During exercise, your muscles draw from these stored carbohydrates. The key idea is that a pattern of consistent fueling matters more than what you eat in a single meal. A low-carb meal before practice won't ruin your performance, and one high-carb meal doesn't guarantee it.

How Does the Body Use Carbohydrates During Exercise?

The harder and faster you exercise, the more your body depends on carbohydrates.

During activities like:

  • Basketball
  • Soccer
  • Volleyball
  • Football
  • Track
  • Wrestling
  • Tennis
  • High-intensity workouts

your muscles quickly use stored glycogen for energy.

As exercise continues, your glycogen stores gradually decrease. If they become too low, your body struggles to keep producing energy fast enough.

Athletes often describe this feeling as "hitting the wall" or running out of gas.

Personal Connection: When your carbohydrate stores are full, you can sprint harder, react faster, make better decisions, and finish practices with more energy.

What Happens When You Don't Eat Enough Carbohydrates?

If your body doesn't receive enough carbohydrates, it cannot fully replace the glycogen used during previous workouts.

Over time, this leads to fuel depletion, meaning your muscles no longer have enough stored energy to perform at their best.

Common signs of carbohydrate depletion include:

  • Feeling tired early during activity
  • Heavy or weak legs
  • Slower sprint speed
  • Poor endurance
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Irritability
  • Feeling mentally "foggy"
  • Needing more rest between drills
  • Reduced motivation to exercise

Because your brain also depends on glucose, inadequate carbohydrate intake can affect more than athletic performance.

You may notice:

  • Trouble paying attention in class
  • Slower reaction time
  • Difficulty remembering information
  • Mood changes
  • Feeling sleepy throughout the day

Personal Connection: You might blame yourself for having a "bad practice," when the real issue is that your body simply didn't have enough fuel.

What Does the Body Do When Carbohydrates Are Too Low?

When carbohydrate stores become depleted, your body must find another way to make energy.

It begins relying more heavily on stored fat and may even break down some muscle protein to help meet energy needs.

Although fat provides energy, it cannot supply energy as quickly as carbohydrates during high-intensity exercise.

This means you may experience:

  • Slower movements
  • Earlier fatigue
  • Lower power output
  • Less explosive jumping and sprinting
  • Harder recovery after workouts

If inadequate carbohydrate intake continues for weeks or months, athletes may struggle to improve their performance despite training hard.

Personal Connection: You can work just as hard as everyone else, but without enough carbohydrates, your body cannot fully support your effort.

How Can You Prevent Carbohydrate Depletion?

The best way to avoid fuel depletion is to eat carbohydrates consistently throughout the day instead of waiting until right before practice.

Healthy carbohydrate choices include:

  • Fruit
  • Whole-grain bread
  • Brown rice
  • Oatmeal
  • Whole-grain cereal
  • Sweet potatoes
  • Potatoes
  • Beans
  • Yogurt
  • Pasta

Eating carbohydrates at breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks helps keep your body's fuel stores ready.

Before exercise, eating carbohydrates provides energy for the upcoming activity.

After exercise, eating carbohydrates helps replace the glycogen your muscles used during the workout.

Pairing carbohydrates with protein after activity can also support muscle recovery.

Personal Connection: Consistent fueling helps you feel stronger during practice, recover faster afterward, and be ready to perform again the next day.

Building a Healthy Carbohydrate Habit

Healthy athletes don't wait until they feel exhausted before thinking about nutrition.

Instead, they build simple daily habits such as:

  • Eating breakfast before school.
  • Including a carbohydrate source with each meal.
  • Choosing a healthy snack before practice when needed.
  • Refueling after exercise with carbohydrates and protein.
  • Drinking enough water throughout the day.

Small habits repeated every day help keep your body's fuel tank full.

Personal Connection: These habits can improve your energy, help you stay focused in class, support a positive mood, and allow you to perform closer to your potential during practices and games.

The Bottom Line

Carbohydrates are your body's primary fuel source. They provide the glucose your muscles and brain need to perform at their best. Your body stores extra carbohydrates as glycogen, which acts as a reserve fuel supply during exercise.

When you don't eat enough carbohydrates, those fuel stores become depleted. As a result, you may feel tired sooner, lose focus, perform below your ability, and recover more slowly.

The good news is that carbohydrate depletion is preventable. By eating balanced meals and snacks that include healthy carbohydrates throughout the day, you give your body the energy it needs to learn, compete, recover, and feel your best—both in the classroom and during athletic performance.

Quiz Carbohydrates and Fueling Quiz 25 min

Passing score: 70%

1. A student skips breakfast and feels fine at first, but during second-period PE they feel slow, tired, and struggle to finish warm-ups. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their muscles are growing faster than normal
  • B. Their body is low on available glucose for energy Correct
  • C. They are overhydrated
  • D. Their protein levels are too high

2. A student eats only a small salad for lunch before basketball practice and feels exhausted halfway through drills. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their fat intake is too high
  • B. Their body is running low on stored carbohydrate fuel Correct
  • C. Their hydration is improving performance too much
  • D. Their body is producing extra glycogen

3. A student eats breakfast but skips lunch before a soccer game. During the second half, they feel slow and cannot sprint like usual. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their glycogen stores are becoming depleted Correct
  • B. Their body has excess energy stored
  • C. Their protein is being used too quickly
  • D. Their digestion is too fast

4. A student eats a carbohydrate-rich meal 2 hours before practice and maintains steady energy throughout training. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their hydration has replaced carbohydrate needs
  • B. Their body has adequate glucose and glycogen stores for fuel Correct
  • C. Their protein intake is preventing fatigue
  • D. Their energy is unrelated to food intake

5. A student feels fine at the start of practice but becomes irritable, tired, and unfocused halfway through. They skipped breakfast and had no snack. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is overheating
  • B. Their muscles are overdeveloped
  • C. Their brain is receiving less glucose for focus and mood regulation Correct
  • D. Their protein levels are too high

6. A student eats only protein before practice and feels sluggish during sprint drills. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their hydration is too high
  • B. Their glycogen stores are fully loaded
  • C. Their body lacks quick-access carbohydrate fuel Correct
  • D. Their muscles are producing excess glucose

7. A student eats consistently throughout the day and performs well in practice from start to finish. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is avoiding all fuel use
  • B. Their carbohydrate intake is supporting stable energy levels Correct
  • C. Their fat is the only energy source being used
  • D. Their muscles are not working hard enough

8. A student reports heavy legs, slow reaction time, and trouble keeping up during practice. They have not eaten since lunch. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein intake is too high
  • B. Their hydration is increasing fatigue
  • C. Their glycogen stores are becoming too low for high-intensity movement Correct
  • D. Their body is producing extra glucose

9. A student eats a banana and granola bar before practice and notices better endurance and focus. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein is replacing glycogen
  • B. Their carbohydrate intake is improving glucose availability for energy Correct
  • C. Their hydration is eliminating fatigue completely
  • D. Their muscles are growing instantly

10. A student trains hard daily but often skips meals and notices their performance is getting worse over time. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their body is unable to consistently replenish glycogen stores Correct
  • B. Their fat intake is too low
  • C. Their hydration is increasing fatigue
  • D. Their muscles are becoming too efficient

11. A student eats a large breakfast with carbohydrates but still feels tired during practice because they skipped lunch. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein intake canceled out energy use
  • B. Their hydration caused fatigue
  • C. Their morning glycogen was not enough to fully fuel later activity Correct
  • D. Their body no longer uses carbohydrates

12. A student feels mentally foggy and struggles to focus during class after a morning workout without eating. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their brain is low on glucose needed for focus and cognitive function Correct
  • B. Their hydration is too high
  • C. Their muscles are too strong
  • D. Their fat stores are overactive

13. A student eats regularly and notices they recover faster after workouts compared to days they skip meals. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their hydration replaces energy needs
  • B. Their protein is stopping all fatigue
  • C. Their carbohydrate intake is helping restore glycogen stores quicker Correct
  • D. Their body is not using energy

14. A student feels fine during light activity but struggles during sprinting and high-intensity drills after not eating enough earlier. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein is too high
  • B. Their body has enough slow energy but not enough fast carbohydrate fuel Correct
  • C. Their hydration is too low
  • D. Their muscles are overtrained

15. A student starts practicing with full energy after eating a balanced meal and finishes practice still feeling strong. What is most likely happening?

  • A. Their protein replaced all energy use
  • B. Their carbohydrate stores supported both immediate and sustained energy needs Correct
  • C. Their body did not use any fuel
  • D. Their hydration eliminated fatigue completely
